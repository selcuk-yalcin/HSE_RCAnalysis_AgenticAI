OLAY ÖZETİ

During scheduled maintenance isolation of heat exchanger E-401B, a process technician opened a bypass valve without confirming upstream block valve closure. Hot crude oil at 220°C and 14 bar was released, ignited by adjacent hot work, resulting in a flash fire that killed two workers and seriously injured four others.

1. YÖNETİCİ ÖZETİ
1.1 Olay Özeti
On 12 January 2026 at 09:47, process technician James Cooper opened a 4-inch bypass equalisation valve on heat exchanger E-401B while the upstream block valve XV-4012 was only 40% closed due to a known actuator fault. This released crude oil fraction at 220°C and 14 bar, creating a vapour cloud that drifted 8 metres to an adjacent welding team performing hot work without proper area clearance. The vapour ignited, causing a flash fire and explosion that engulfed a 20-metre radius.
Greenfield Refinery Ltd, Unit 4 Crude Distillation Unit, Heat Exchanger Bay 7, United Kingdom
Two fatalities: James Cooper (41, process technician) and Ryan Mills (29, contract welder). Four workers sustained serious burns and blast injuries requiring ICU admission. Two workers self-evacuated with minor injuries.
Emergency alarm was manually activated at 09:48. On-site fire brigade arrived within 5 minutes and commenced fire suppression. Both fatalities were confirmed by 10:15. Four critically injured workers were evacuated to hospital by 10:30. Site emergency was declared at 11:00, and HSE Executive and police were notified immediately. Full site shutdown was implemented, all active permits were cancelled, and external independent investigation team was engaged.
1.2 Temel Bulgular
  • Isolation checklist was only 6 of 12 steps completed; physical valve verification was not performed before opening pressurised line
  • Critical isolation valve XV-4012 had a known actuator fault for 3 months, achieving only 40% closure, with no interim risk assessment or compensatory measures
  • All three gas detectors in Bay 7 were offline for 6 weeks prior to incident; no temporary gas monitoring or area restrictions were implemented
  • Two conflicting permits (cold work PTW-1847 and hot work HWP-0392) were raised independently with no SIMOPS conflict check performed

3. ANALİZ YÖNTEMİ - 5 WHY
3.1 5-Why Tekniği
The 5-Why technique was applied rigorously to each causal branch to prevent superficial conclusions and ensure true root causes were identified. For each immediate cause, the investigation team asked 'Why did this happen?' iteratively, using documentary evidence and witness testimony to validate each answer. The technique was stopped when answers pointed to management decisions or systemic organisational factors beyond the control of frontline workers. For example, Branch 1 traced incomplete isolation checklists through four 'Why' levels to reveal normalisation of deviance as the root cause. Branch 2 traced a defective valve through budget deferrals to reveal deferred maintenance as an accepted norm. Branch 3 traced offline gas detectors to production-priority decision-making. Each chain was cross-validated against multiple evidence sources (maintenance logs, witness statements, management meeting minutes, KPI data) to ensure robustness and avoid investigator bias.

4. CRITICAL FACTOR 1 — BEHAVIOURAL CAUSE: INCOMPLETE ISOLATION PROCEDURE
4.1 Başlangıç Durumu ve Doğrudan Neden
Process technician James Cooper, with 14 years of service and extensive experience in heat exchanger isolations, was assigned to isolate heat exchanger E-401B for scheduled maintenance under Permit to Work PTW-1847. The isolation procedure required completion of a 12-step checklist, including physical verification of all block valve positions before opening any bypass or drain valves. Cooper had performed this task dozens of times previously without incident. On the morning of 12 January 2026, Cooper and colleague Tom Brady commenced the isolation at 08:00. By 09:30, Cooper reported to the control room that the isolation 'looked complete,' but the isolation checklist showed only 6 of 12 steps signed off. Critically, the step requiring physical verification of upstream block valve XV-4012 closure was not completed. At 09:40, Cooper opened the 4-inch bypass equalisation valve, believing the upstream line to be isolated based on verbal confirmation from the control room operator who stated the valve 'should be closed.' No physical verification was performed.
Individual rule/procedure violation: Cooper opened the bypass valve without completing the isolation checklist and without physically verifying that the upstream block valve XV-4012 was fully closed
4.2 5-Why Analiz Zinciri
NEDEN 1
Why did James Cooper open the bypass equalisation valve without physically verifying that upstream block valve XV-4012 was closed?
→ When Cooper called the control room, the operator stated the valve 'should be closed,' and Cooper accepted this verbal confirmation as sufficient. He did not perform physical verification. Additionally, he had completed only 6 of 12 steps on the isolation checklist, meaning he had skipped the valve position verification step entirely.
A - Individual/Behavioural
NEDEN 2
Why was Cooper able to proceed with the work using an incomplete checklist and without physical verification?
→ Incomplete checklists were routinely accepted on site. According to witness Tom Brady's statement, 'supervisors never rejected a job for incomplete paperwork.' They had performed this job dozens of times before without issues, and procedural violations had become normalised practice.
C - Workplace
NEDEN 3
Why were incomplete isolation checklists and procedural shortcuts consistently accepted by supervisors without correction?
→ Deviations had become normalised across the facility (normalisation of deviance). As documented in the report, management tolerated known deviations; facility KPIs focused on production uptime, and safety was tracked only through lagging indicators. Supervisors routinely approved incomplete checklists.
D - Organisational
NEDEN 4
Why did management not intervene to enforce procedural compliance before these deviations became normalised?
→ The facility-level safety culture was production-focused. The last process safety audit was conducted 18 months ago (exceeding the company standard of 12 months). The safety-critical maintenance backlog contained over 140 open work orders, including 12 critical defects, that had been pending for over 6 months, and senior management had deferred budget approvals. Management systematically tolerated known deviations.
D - Organisational
4.3 Kök Neden
KÖK NEDEN 1: Normalisation of Deviance in Isolation Procedures
[D1.5 / Organisational] At Greenfield Refinery, incomplete isolation checklists, commencement of work without physical valve verification, and skipping of safety-critical procedural steps had been systematically accepted and normalised by supervisors and management. According to Tom Brady's witness statement, 'supervisors never rejected a job for incomplete paperwork,' and this work had been performed 'dozens of times without issues.' In this normalised deviance environment, Cooper opened the bypass valve without physical verification despite the known actuator fault in XV-4012. This root cause reflects a deep organisational culture failure where procedural compliance was not enforced, deviations were tolerated as routine, and production priorities consistently overrode safety discipline.
4.4 Organizasyonel Faktörler
  • Facility KPIs were focused on production uptime; safety was measured only through lagging indicators, creating implicit pressure to prioritise production over procedural compliance
  • The last process safety audit was conducted 18 months prior to the incident, exceeding the company standard of 12 months, indicating inadequate oversight of safety management systems
  • Supervisors routinely approved incomplete isolation checklists without challenge or correction, signalling to workers that procedural shortcuts were acceptable
  • Management was aware of the 140+ item maintenance backlog (including 12 safety-critical defects) and deferred budget approvals for over 6 months, demonstrating systemic tolerance of known safety risks
5. CRITICAL FACTOR 2 — CONDITION CAUSE: DEFECTIVE CRITICAL ISOLATION VALVE
5.1 Başlangıç Durumu ve Doğrudan Neden
Block valve XV-4012 was designated as a CRITICAL isolation valve on the site Piping & Instrumentation Diagram (P&ID) for heat exchanger E-401B. This valve was the primary barrier preventing crude oil fraction at 220°C and 14 bar from entering the bypass line during maintenance isolation. On 14 October 2025, three months before the incident, an actuator fault was identified and logged in the maintenance system. The fault caused the valve to achieve only 40% closure instead of full closure. Despite being classified as a safety-critical component, the fault was simply added to the maintenance backlog with no interim risk assessment, no temporary compensatory measures (such as installation of a line blind), and no restriction on continued operation. The valve remained in this defective state for three months. When Cooper opened the bypass equalisation valve on 12 January 2026, the defective XV-4012 allowed high-pressure, high-temperature crude oil to flow into the bypass line, creating the hydrocarbon release that led to the fatal flash fire.
Equipment/facility failure: Critical isolation valve XV-4012 actuator fault allowed only 40% closure, permitting crude oil fraction at 220°C and 14 bar to be released through the bypass line
5.2 5-Why Analiz Zinciri
NEDEN 1
Why was the critical isolation valve XV-4012 actuator fault left unrepaired for 3 months?
→ The XV-4012 actuator fault was logged in the maintenance system on 14 October 2025 but was only added to the maintenance backlog. No interim risk assessment was conducted for the fault, and no temporary compensatory measures (such as installation of a line blind) were implemented.
C - Workplace
NEDEN 2
Why was the fault in XV-4012, designated as a CRITICAL isolation valve on the P&ID, not prioritised as a safety-critical maintenance item?
→ The facility had accumulated over 140 open maintenance items, of which 12 were safety-critical defects. Budget approval for instrument repairs had been deferred by senior management for over 6 months and postponed to Q2 2026. As a result, even safety-critical equipment faults were queued in the routine maintenance backlog.
D - Organisational
NEDEN 3
Why was the budget required for safety-critical equipment repairs deferred for over 6 months?
→ Senior management had set facility KPIs focused on production uptime, and safety indicators consisted only of lagging metrics. Despite Safety Manager Ian Pearce reporting the gas detector faults and maintenance backlog to senior management in November 2025, budget approval was not granted.
D - Organisational
NEDEN 4
Why did senior management not allocate budget and intervene despite knowing the size and risk of the safety-critical maintenance backlog?
→ Facility management had made the deferral of safety-critical equipment repairs a known and accepted practice. The process safety audit had not been conducted for 18 months (exceeding the company standard of 12 months), the risk created by deferring safety-critical maintenance items was not addressed at board level, and these deferrals were systematically tolerated.
D - Organisational
5.3 Kök Neden
KÖK NEDEN 2: Deferred Maintenance Accepted as Organisational Norm
[D6.6 / Organisational] Greenfield Refinery senior management deferred 12 safety-critical maintenance items, including the actuator fault in critical isolation valve XV-4012 (as designated on the P&ID), within a backlog of over 140 open maintenance items for more than 6 months. Instrument repair budget was postponed to Q2 2026. During this deferral period, no interim risk assessment or compensatory measures (such as line blinds or area restrictions) were required. This situation resulted in the operation of safety-critical equipment in a defective state becoming an institutionally accepted norm. The root cause reflects a systemic failure in asset integrity management where financial and production priorities consistently overrode the imperative to maintain safety-critical barriers in functional condition.
5.4 Organizasyonel Faktörler
  • Senior management deferred instrument repair budget for over 6 months despite being informed by the Safety Manager of the growing backlog and specific safety-critical defects
  • The 140+ item maintenance backlog, including 12 safety-critical defects, was known to senior management but not escalated to board level for risk assessment and resource allocation
  • No interim risk assessment or compensatory measures were mandated for safety-critical equipment operating in a defective state, indicating absence of a formal Management of Change (MOC) process for degraded safety barriers
  • Process safety audits were overdue by 6 months (18 months since last audit vs. 12-month company standard), demonstrating inadequate governance oversight of asset integrity and maintenance management systems
6. CRITICAL FACTOR 3 — CONDITION CAUSE: FAILED GAS DETECTION SYSTEM
6.1 Başlangıç Durumu ve Doğrudan Neden
Bay 7 of the Crude Distillation Unit was equipped with three fixed gas detectors (HD-407, HD-408, HD-409) designed to provide automatic detection and alarm for hydrocarbon vapour releases. These detectors were safety-critical instruments forming part of the facility's process safety barriers. On 29 November 2025, six weeks before the incident, all three gas detectors in Bay 7 were found to be faulty and went offline. The faults were logged in the maintenance system. However, no repairs were carried out, and the detectors remained offline. Critically, no temporary compensatory measures were implemented during this period — no portable gas monitors were deployed, no continuous manual gas testing was required, and no access restrictions were placed on Bay 7. When the hydrocarbon vapour cloud was released at 09:43 on 12 January 2026, there was no automatic detection or alarm. Workers in the area, including welder Ryan Mills, had no early warning of the hazardous atmosphere. Mills noticed the smell of hydrocarbons at 09:45 but continued working, having received no stop-work authority training. The vapour ignited two minutes later.
Defective or absent warning/alarm systems: All gas detectors in Bay 7 (HD-407, HD-408, HD-409) were faulty and offline, preventing detection of the hydrocarbon vapour cloud and generation of automatic alarm
6.2 5-Why Analiz Zinciri
NEDEN 1
Why were the gas detectors in Bay 7 (HD-407, HD-408, HD-409) offline and unable to detect the hydrocarbon vapour cloud?
→ The gas detector faults were logged in the maintenance system on 29 November 2025 (6 weeks before the incident), but repairs were not carried out and the detectors remained offline.
B - Job/Condition
NEDEN 2
Why were the gas detectors not repaired despite the fault being logged 6 weeks earlier?
→ Budget approval for instrument repairs had been deferred by senior management to the second quarter of 2026. According to Safety Manager Ian Pearce's statement, the gas detector fault was reported in November, but budget approval was not granted.
D - Organisational
NEDEN 3
Why were no temporary compensatory measures (portable gas monitors, area restrictions, continuous gas testing) implemented while the gas detectors were offline?
→ As explicitly stated in the report, despite the gas detectors being offline, no temporary compensatory measures were implemented and no access restrictions were imposed on the area. No interim risk assessment was conducted for the defective safety-critical equipment.
D - Organisational
NEDEN 4
Why was no interim risk assessment conducted and no compensatory measure requirement triggered while safety-critical gas detectors were offline?
→ The facility had over 140 open maintenance items, including 12 safety-critical defects. Despite senior management being aware of this situation, no budget was allocated and repair of safety-critical instrument faults was deferred. Facility KPIs focused on production uptime, and safety indicators were tracked only through lagging metrics.
D - Organisational
6.3 Kök Neden
KÖK NEDEN 3: Production Pressure Overriding Safety-Critical Expenditure
[D1.4 / Organisational] Senior management deferred budget approval for safety-critical instrument repairs (including gas detectors) for over 6 months in favour of production continuity, focused facility KPIs on production uptime, and knowingly did not fund a maintenance backlog of over 140 items including 12 safety-critical defects. This situation directly led to the gas detectors in Bay 7 remaining offline and unrepaired for 6 weeks, with no compensatory measures implemented. The root cause reflects a conscious management prioritisation decision where financial and production objectives systematically overrode investment in safety-critical systems, rather than a generic resource shortage.
6.4 Organizasyonel Faktörler
  • Senior management deferred instrument repair budget to Q2 2026 despite being informed of safety-critical gas detector failures, demonstrating a conscious decision to prioritise financial targets over safety barrier integrity
  • Facility KPIs were explicitly focused on production uptime, with safety measured only through lagging indicators, creating systemic pressure to avoid production interruptions for safety repairs
  • No Management of Change (MOC) process was triggered when safety-critical gas detectors went offline, indicating absence of a formal system to assess and mitigate degraded safety barriers
  • Senior management was aware of the 140+ item maintenance backlog (including 12 safety-critical defects) but did not escalate to board level or allocate emergency funding, demonstrating institutional tolerance of degraded safety-critical systems
6. KATKIDA BULUNAN FAKTÖRLER
Faktör Türü Açıklama Etki Seviyesi
Simultaneous Operations (SIMOPS) Management Failure Two conflicting permits (cold work PTW-1847 for heat exchanger isolation and hot work HWP-0392 for welding on adjacent naphtha line) were raised independently by different supervisors with no conflict check performed. The facility had no enforced SIMOPS policy, and the SIMOPS coordination board was empty. This allowed ignition sources to be present in an area where hydrocarbon release was possible, creating the conditions for the flash fire. Critical
Inadequate Permit to Work (PTW) System The PTW system allowed concurrent conflicting permits to be issued without automated or manual conflict checking. The isolation checklist for PTW-1847 was incomplete (6 of 12 steps), yet the permit was not suspended or challenged. No requirement for continuous gas monitoring was specified in either permit despite the proximity of hot work to hydrocarbon systems. Physical valve verification was not enforced as a mandatory hold point before opening pressurised lines. Critical
Absence of Stop-Work Authority and Empowerment Welder Ryan Mills noticed the smell of hydrocarbons at 09:45 but continued working, having received no training on stop-work authority or empowerment to halt unsafe operations. Workers across the site had not been trained to recognise that they had the right and responsibility to stop work in the presence of uncontrolled hazards. This absence of stop-work culture was a direct contributing factor to the fatalities. High
Inadequate Contractor Management Contract welder Ryan Mills had received only a 1-day site induction with no process safety awareness training specific to hydrocarbon release hazards, SIMOPS risks, or emergency response procedures. Contractor competency verification and site-specific hazard training were inadequate. The contractor management system did not ensure that contractors working in high-hazard areas received equivalent safety training to direct employees. High
Communication and Shift Handover Failures The planned maintenance activity and the known fault in valve XV-4012 were communicated verbally only during the night shift handover at 06:00, with no written shift transfer notes. Critical information about the defective valve and the need for enhanced verification was not transferred to the day shift. The control room operator who told Cooper that XV-4012 'should be closed' was unaware of the actuator fault and assumed a compensatory line blind had been installed. High
Inadequate Emergency Preparedness The emergency alarm was activated manually by a bystander at 09:48, one minute after ignition, rather than automatically by the gas detection system (which was offline). There was no emergency muster or evacuation procedure specific to hydrocarbon release in Bay 7. On-site fire brigade response was effective (5 minutes), but the absence of automatic detection and alarm meant that workers had no early warning to evacuate before ignition. Medium
Inadequate Human Factors and Ergonomics The isolation checklist was a paper-based form that could be partially completed and submitted without triggering any system alert or supervisory review. There was no forcing function (such as a digital checklist with mandatory hold points) to prevent work proceeding with incomplete isolation verification. The design of the work process did not account for human error or intentional non-compliance. Medium
Inadequate Process Safety Culture The facility exhibited multiple indicators of weak process safety culture: production-focused KPIs, tolerance of procedural non-compliance, deferred safety-critical maintenance, overdue process safety audits, and absence of stop-work authority. Workers and supervisors had learned that 'getting the job done' was more valued than procedural compliance, and that raising safety concerns would not result in meaningful action. Critical

8. ÇIKARILAN DERSLER
NE YAPILMALI
• Always perform physical verification of valve positions before opening any pressurised line, regardless of verbal confirmation from control room or previous experience. Physical verification must be an independent check performed by a second competent person and documented as a mandatory hold point in the isolation checklist. • Never accept incomplete isolation checklists or procedural shortcuts, even if the work has been performed successfully many times before. Procedural compliance is not optional — it is the primary defence against low-probability, high-consequence events. Supervisors must reject incomplete work and reinforce the message that safety-critical steps cannot be skipped. • Always check for conflicting permits and simultaneous operations (SIMOPS) before commencing work. If you are performing maintenance or isolation work, verify that no hot work or other ignition sources are present in the area. If you are performing hot work, verify that no hydrocarbon release is possible in the area. SIMOPS conflicts must be identified and resolved before work starts. • Exercise stop-work authority immediately if you observe an uncontrolled hazard, even if you are not directly involved in the work. If you smell hydrocarbons, see a vapour cloud, or observe any unsafe condition, stop work, evacuate the area, and raise the alarm. You have the right and responsibility to stop work, and you will be protected from retaliation.
UZUN VADELİ ÇÖZÜMLER
• Develop and sustain a process safety culture where procedural compliance is valued and reinforced, and where production pressure never overrides safety-critical decision-making. This requires visible leadership commitment, balanced KPIs that measure safety culture health, and consistent consequences for non-compliance at all levels of the organisation. • Implement a robust asset integrity management system where safety-critical equipment is maintained in functional condition, defects are repaired promptly, and interim risk assessments and compensatory measures are mandatory for any degraded safety barrier. Safety-critical maintenance must be ring-fenced from budget deferrals and production pressures. • Establish independent governance oversight of process safety through regular audits, board-level process safety committees, and external expert review. Major accident risk must be visible to senior management and the board, and decisions to accept residual risk must be made at an appropriate level with full understanding of consequences. • Invest in human factors and error prevention through digital work management systems, forcing functions in critical procedures, independent verification of safety-critical tasks, and continuous improvement based on near-miss learning. The work system must be designed to prevent human error, not to rely on perfect human performance.
İLETİŞİM VE PAYLAŞIM
• Communicate the findings of this investigation to all site personnel through face-to-face briefings, ensuring that every worker understands the causal factors, the root causes, and the lessons learned. The communication must be honest and transparent, acknowledging organisational failures and leadership accountability, not blaming frontline workers. • Share this incident report and lessons learned across all company facilities and with industry peers through process safety networks and forums. The systemic failures identified in this investigation (normalisation of deviance, deferred maintenance, production pressure) are common across the industry, and sharing lessons can prevent similar tragedies elsewhere. • Establish a regular communication cadence for process safety performance, including safety-critical equipment status, maintenance backlog, near-miss trends, and culture survey results. This information must be communicated to the workforce, to site leadership, and to the board on a monthly basis to maintain visibility and accountability.
EĞİTİM VE FARKINDALIK
• Develop and deliver comprehensive process safety awareness training for all personnel (employees and contractors) working in high-hazard areas. Training must cover hydrocarbon release hazards, ignition sources, SIMOPS risks, emergency response, stop-work authority, and the consequences of procedural non-compliance. Training must be scenario-based and include realistic case studies such as this incident. • Implement competency-based training and verification for all safety-critical tasks, including isolation procedures, permit issuance, SIMOPS coordination, and physical valve verification. Competency must be assessed through practical demonstration, not just classroom attendance, and must be refreshed annually. • Deliver leadership training for all supervisors and managers on process safety culture, visible safety leadership, and the importance of challenging non-compliance. Leaders must understand that their actions and decisions set the tone for the organisation, and that tolerating procedural shortcuts or deferring safety-critical expenditure sends a powerful message that safety is not genuinely valued. • Provide human factors training for engineers and designers to ensure that future work processes, procedures, and systems are designed to prevent human error through forcing functions, independent verification, and error-tolerant design, rather than relying on perfect human performance.
10. SONUÇ VE ÖNERİLER
10.1 Genel Değerlendirme
The fatal flash fire and explosion at Greenfield Refinery on 12 January 2026 was not an unforeseeable accident or an isolated human error. It was the inevitable consequence of systemic organisational failures that had developed and been tolerated over an extended period. The investigation identified three distinct but interconnected root causes, all at the organisational level: normalisation of deviance in isolation procedures (D1.5), deferred maintenance accepted as an organisational norm (D6.6), and production pressure overriding safety-critical expenditure (D1.4). These root causes reflect a fundamental failure of leadership, governance, and process safety culture. The immediate causes — an incomplete isolation checklist, a defective critical isolation valve, and offline gas detectors — were symptoms of these deeper organisational failures, not independent events. The investigation found clear evidence that procedural non-compliance was routinely accepted by supervisors, that safety-critical equipment defects were knowingly deferred for months without interim risk assessment or compensatory measures, and that senior management prioritised production and financial targets over investment in safety-critical systems. The facility's KPI structure, overdue process safety audits, and absence of board-level oversight of major accident risk created an environment where these failures could develop unchecked. The human cost of these failures was catastrophic: two workers lost their lives, four sustained life-threatening injuries, and families and communities were devastated. This incident was preventable. Every causal factor identified in this investigation was known to the organisation before the incident occurred, and every corrective action recommended in this report could have been implemented proactively to prevent the tragedy. The investigation team concludes that Greenfield Refinery was operating with an unacceptable level of major accident risk, and that this risk was not visible to or understood by senior management and the board. The corrective actions recommended in this report are comprehensive and address all identified root causes and contributing factors. However, the investigation team emphasises that sustainable improvement will require a fundamental change in organisational culture and leadership commitment. Process safety must be recognised as a core business value, not a compliance burden. Safety-critical systems must be maintained in functional condition, and production must never override safety-critical decision-making. Workers must be empowered to stop work in the presence of uncontrolled hazards, and leaders must be held accountable for process safety performance. The investigation team recommends that this incident be treated as a sentinel event for the entire organisation, and that the lessons learned be applied across all company facilities to prevent recurrence.
